🧠 The Psychology of Decision-Making

ĐOẠN A

Traditional economic theory has long posited that humans are rational actors who weigh costs and benefits to maximise utility. However, decades of behavioural research have revealed that real-world decision-making deviates systematically from this idealised model. People frequently rely on mental shortcuts, are swayed by irrelevant contextual factors, and exhibit predictable irrationalities that challenge the notion of the purely logical decision-maker. This realisation has forced economists and psychologists to reconsider the very foundations of human choice.

ĐOẠN B

A substantial body of literature documents the numerous biases that influence human judgement. For example, the anchoring effect occurs when an initial piece of information exerts undue weight on subsequent decisions, even when that anchor is arbitrary. Likewise, confirmation bias leads individuals to seek out and interpret evidence that supports pre-existing beliefs while disregarding contradictory data. These and other cognitive distortions operate largely below conscious awareness, yet they profoundly affect everything from financial investments to medical diagnoses, underscoring the fallibility of human reasoning.

ĐOẠN C

Contrary to the classical view that emotions are obstacles to sound judgement, contemporary neuroscience suggests that affective states are integral to the decision-making process. Studies of patients with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex—a region critical for integrating emotional signals—reveal that such individuals often make disastrous choices despite having intact logical reasoning abilities. This paradox demonstrates that feelings and rationality are not opposing forces but rather complementary components of a unified system for navigating complex choices.

ĐOẠN D

These scientific findings have not remained confined to academic journals; they have been enthusiastically adopted by policymakers and marketers alike. Governments around the world have established behavioural insight units—colloquially known as 'nudge units'—to design interventions that steer citizens toward better choices without restricting their freedom. In the commercial sphere, companies use subtle environmental cues to influence consumer behaviour, from the strategic placement of products on supermarket shelves to the default settings in online forms. Such applications demonstrate the practical power of understanding how people actually make decisions.
